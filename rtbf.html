The Siren Song of Oblivion: Why Europe’s digital eraser threatens the public record

Reminder of our discussion question: Should a person be allowed to delete their online history? Is the "Right to Be Forgotten" an absolute right?

(Adapted from The Economist and The Guardian)

The grand ambition of the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) was to grant data sovereignty to the citizen, transforming the individual from a mere data point into a rights-holder. At the heart of this project lies the so-called "Right to be Forgotten," or more accurately, the "Right to Erasure." It is a noble, profoundly human impulse—the desire for a fresh start, untethered from the digital permanence of a youthful indiscretion or a resolved criminal conviction. Yet, six years after the GDPR’s full implementation, this right has proven itself not a tool of human rehabilitation, but a regulatory anomaly that has outsourced judicial review to Silicon Valley and introduced a troubling opacity to the historical record.

The Private Arbiter of History

The most acute paradox of the droit à l'oubli remains the delegation of its enforcement. When an individual seeks to have a link concerning their past—be it a decades-old auction notice of a repossessed home or a report on a criminal conviction—removed from search results, the ultimate arbiter is not a magistrate, but an American tech giant. Google, and its competitors, are forced to operate as a decentralised supreme court, balancing the claimant’s right to privacy against the public interest in accessing that information.

This administrative fiction has created a sclerotic regulatory regime. The power to determine what constitutes "inadequate, irrelevant, or no longer relevant" data is wielded by private compliance teams, whose core incentive is risk minimisation. The path of least resistance often involves erasure, particularly when confronted with the complexity of content relating to individuals who are neither unambiguously public figures nor anonymous citizens. The regulation thus compels Big Tech to exert editorial control over information it did not create, a position for which it is ill-suited and ethically compromised.

The Illusion of Global Scope

The inherent borderlessness of the internet has exposed the fragility of European regulatory ambition. Early on, European regulators, led most fiercely by France’s Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL), sought to have delisting orders applied globally, demanding that search results be scrubbed from all Google domains, including the ubiquitous google.com. This attempt to export European law—the creation of a droit à l'oubli that respected no geographical fence-was always destined for conflict.

The European Court of Justice (ECJ) eventually intervened in 2019, ruling that while search engines must implement the right within the EU via geoblocking to "seriously discourage" local users from accessing the content, they had no obligation to apply the removal worldwide. This decision, hailed by free-speech advocates, confirmed the limits of digital sovereignty. Europe, in essence, succeeded only in constructing a localised "Swiss cheese internet," a fragmented search index where the public record is viewable only if the browser sits outside the bloc, or if one is moderately tech-savvy.

The Veil of Silence

Most recently, the regulatory environment has darkened further, particularly for the journalistic estate. A 2024 ruling by the Swedish data protection authority—which is now being applied across the EU—found that notifying publishers when one of their links has been delisted constitutes a further breach of the claimant’s privacy.

The practical consequence is that search engines now delist content quietly, offering no mechanism for the original publisher, who often holds the public-interest argument, to challenge the removal. This systemic opacity is precisely what enables "serial miscreants"—criminals, disgraced professionals, and fraudsters—to systematically sanitise their digital past, hiding legitimate reporting from the public square without due scrutiny.

The intent behind the right to be forgotten is, undeniably, humane. But in its execution, Europe has created a peculiar regulatory regime: an expensive legal edifice that imposes censorship by obscurity, forces private corporations to police the public record, and risks empowering the very individuals who should remain subject to the necessary scrutiny of an unforgiving, yet truthful, history. Forgetting, it turns out, is a privilege that can only be purchased at the cost of collective memory.

The Guardian (read on PressReader), February 2024

Google stops notifying publishers of ‘right to be forgotten’ removals from search results

Same news as the last section of our text. Google no longer tells publishers when a link to their article is delisted, following a Swedish data protection decision. Journalists say this makes it harder to spot people who abuse the rules to hide legitimate reporting.

Think about it: Compare: who does the author blame, the regulator, Google or the claimants? PressReader may ask you to sign in; the page is the Guardian’s own piece.

PBS NewsHour, 29 June 2015

BBC responds to privacy law by publishing list of stories removed from Google search

The BBC decided to keep a public list of its articles that Google has dropped from its results. It argues that its archive is harder to find when pages vanish from searches, even though they stay online. The BBC presents this as a contribution to the policy debate.

Think about it: Link with our text: how can a publisher react to delisting? Is a public list a fair answer?

NPR, 24 September 2019

‘Right To Be Forgotten’ Only Applies To Websites Inside EU, European Court Says

The European Court of Justice ruled that Google must apply removal requests on its European search sites, not worldwide. The decision narrows the 2014 ruling in Google’s favour, although the French regulator says it may still ask for global delisting in some cases.

Think about it: Link with our text: this is the 2019 ruling behind the “Swiss cheese internet”. Which side does NPR seem to give more room to?

Reuters (read on Yahoo), December 2022

Google must remove ‘manifestly inaccurate’ data, EU top court says

Europe’s highest court ruled that Google must delist results when a person can prove that the linked information is clearly false. This proof does not require a court ruling against the publisher. Two executives had asked for links and thumbnail photos to be removed.

Think about it: Link with our text: here the claimant proves that the data is false. Does that change your view on who should decide?
